Where property documentation actually breaks down

  • An asset exists, but the record doesn't — or doesn't match what's actually on the shelf.
  • Custody and location aren't clearly assigned, so no one's sure who's responsible.
  • An asset is surplused or transferred with no closing paperwork, and inventory never catches up.
  • Condition and maintenance history lives in someone's memory, not on paper.
  • When an audit, grant review, or loss event happens, staff reconstruct records after the fact instead of producing them.

What the public record is telling us

The Documentation Center's documentation patterns come from an ongoing research program built on public government audit findings — not assumptions about what a department "should" have. Recurring failure patterns — records that don't exist, identification too vague to match a physical item to a file, disposals with no closing paperwork, safeguarding gaps — show up independently across unrelated departments within the same organization.

Our Government Documentation Research Program is expanding jurisdiction by jurisdiction, using public audit findings to identify recurring property, inventory, and evidence-management gaps.

Our research standard

We distinguish documented findings from our interpretation, publish our sources where we can, name uncertainty plainly, and don't assume every organizational problem is a software problem.

From documented failure to standardized record

A recurring audit finding — an asset that can't be located, or a record too vague to match to the physical item — points to a documentation gap, not a staffing problem. The Documentation Center converts that gap into a standardized record: what it is, where it is, who has custody, its condition, and what happened to it. That's the shift from "we think we know what we have" to "we can produce it."

What the kit structures your team to do

This is a documentation structure, not software — a defined paper and template system your team follows.

  • Identify — what is the asset, and what identifies it specifically enough to match to the physical item?
  • Document — what evidence exists: acquisition, condition, funding source?
  • Associate — who has custody? What location or program does it belong to?
  • Maintain — what changed, and when: condition, transfer, disposal?
  • Organize for retrieval — the binder and template structure is built so your team can find and assemble the record quickly when asked — indexed, tabbed, consistent across departments.

What's in the kit

A physical binder plus structured templates — acquisition records, identification and location templates, condition and maintenance logs, and disposition documentation. Built to work alongside your existing systems, not replace them.

What this is — and isn't

Not:

  • Not an accounting system or ERP replacement.
  • Not a CMMS or asset-management software replacement.
  • Not an audit firm, and not audit representation.
  • Not a grant-management system.
  • Not a software platform — this is a physical documentation system your team runs.
  • Not a promise of a specific audit or compliance outcome.

Is: A documentation structure designed to make property records more consistent, complete, and retrievable when someone asks for them.

Research methodology

We review public audit findings from government oversight bodies, looking for documentation and property-control patterns that recur across unrelated departments rather than appearing as isolated incidents. Where a pattern repeats independently, we treat it as evidence of a real, addressable gap — and we state the size of the sample it rests on rather than implying more. This work is ongoing and expanding jurisdiction by jurisdiction; we say so plainly rather than presenting it as complete.

What our research does not establish

An audit finding does not automatically mean a documentation kit addresses it. Some findings point to policy, training, staffing, or governance changes instead — and a kit would not fix those.

Part of what this research tries to do is tell those apart: distinguishing an actual documentation problem from a problem that merely produces documentation symptoms.
